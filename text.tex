I enjoy
teaching mathematics at all levels and see teaching and dissemination as a central part of mathematics.
In teaching mathematics,
I aim to give students not only a clear and regerious foundation in the materual, but also the intuitve understand that is so hard to learn from
books alone.

During my time at Cambridge,
I have \textit{supervised} several times for the courses Algebraic Topology,
Riemann Surfaces, and Complex Analysis.
Supervisions are Cambridge's version of an exercise class.
In lieu of going through the problems in a standard classroom format,
students meet a supervisor in small groups of two or three.
While the format might be peculiar,
it has taught me much.
In such direct contact with the students,
I was able to get a good feeling for which parts of the courses
are easy to follow, and which ones introduce new ideas that
require the student to go through a paradigm shift in their own thinking.
Such steps take much longer for students to understand.
While each student is different, and learns in their own way and at their own pace,
I was able to see patterns across all students in my supervisions.
When teaching any undergraduate course on related topics in the future,
I will be able to draw on the deeper insight I gained while supervising at Cambridge.

Even before starting my PhD, I was a teaching assistant during my Master's and
undergraduate studies, and gained experience in traditional classroom
teaching there.
At ETH Zurich, I taught example
classes for a series of
Analysis courses,
ranging from Analysis II to Analysis IV.
Analysis II is a first year course in
calculus in several variables. It starts from metric spaces and compactness, then covers multivariable differentiation, the inverse and implicit function theorems, and Riemann integration. It ends with the divergence and Stokes theorems via differential forms and a final unit on ODEs.
Analysis II builds the modern theory of measure and integration, constructing the Lebesgue measure and integral on $\mathbb{R}^n$ as a generalisation of the Riemann integral. It covers the main convergence theorems (monotone convergence, Fatou, dominated convergence), product measures and Fubini, and $L^p$ spaces.
Analysis IV develops Hilbert space theory (bases, projections, Riesz representation), then Fourier series on $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$ and the Fourier transform on $\mathbb{R}^d$, with applications to the heat equation and Hermite functions. It ends with compact and self-adjoint operators and the spectral theorem.
Teaching exercise classes for this sequence of courses increasing in difficulty and
specialization was a rewarding experience.
I specially enjoyed seeing some students progress all the way through these classes,
and witness their mathematical growth.
In my exercise classes, I made sure to focus on the conceptual understanding of the students.
In order to make sure that everyone was always up to speed on the material from the lectures, I encouraged students
to submit questions about the previous lectures ahead of the class
and did a review session
at the beginning of each class.
Next to analyzing the submitted questions to identify any stumbling blocks,
I also used my own experience of learning these materials during my undergraduate degree.
My aim was always to try to give the students a geometric and intuitive understanding of the material.
For this purpose, I prepared computer visualization for key concepts such as the Weierstrass function or the Fourier modes.
These visual elements were very well received by the students, and I plan to keep using them in my teaching.
To make the classes interactive, and an environment in which students were happy
to ask questions and speak up,
I usually ended the class by discussing an easy unseen problem and solve it in class together with the students.
This also allowed me to gauge the understand of the majority in a clear way,
and helped me design the next class as well as give feedback about the students progress to the lecturer.

I aim to always improve my teaching and to take the lessons of my teaching experience from Cambridge and Zurich
to heart.
